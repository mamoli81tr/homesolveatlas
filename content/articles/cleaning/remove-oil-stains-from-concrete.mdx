quickAnswer: "Fresh oil stains lift best with an absorbent material like cat litter or baking soda left overnight, followed by a degreasing dish soap scrub. Old, set-in stains need a dedicated concrete degreaser and more scrubbing time."
symptoms:
  - "A dark, greasy patch on a garage floor or driveway"
  - "Stain that darkens further when it rains or gets wet"
  - "Slippery texture on the stained area"
causes:
  - title: "Motor oil, transmission fluid, or other automotive leaks"
    description: "Concrete is porous, so oil from a leaking or dripping vehicle soaks into the surface rather than sitting on top of it."
  - title: "Grill or cooking grease drips"
    description: "Grease from an outdoor grill or fryer can stain a patio or garage floor in a similar way to automotive oil."
  - title: "Stains left untreated for an extended period"
    description: "The longer oil sits, the deeper it penetrates the concrete's pores, which is why older stains typically need stronger treatment."
steps:
  - title: "Absorb fresh oil first"
    description: "For a recent spill, cover it generously with cat litter, sawdust, or baking soda and let it sit for several hours to overnight, absorbing as much oil as possible before cleaning."
  - title: "Sweep away the absorbent material"
    description: "Sweep up the litter or baking soda, disposing of it in the trash (not down a drain)."
  - title: "Apply a degreaser"
    description: "Pour a concrete-safe degreaser or a strong dish soap directly onto the stain, working it in with a stiff-bristled brush in a circular motion."
  - title: "Let it dwell, then rinse"
    description: "Allow the degreaser to sit for the time stated on its label (typically 10–15 minutes), then rinse thoroughly with a hose."
  - title: "Repeat for old, set-in stains"
    description: "Older stains often need 2–3 rounds of degreasing and scrubbing, and may benefit from a dedicated commercial concrete oil-stain remover for full results."
safeChecks:
  - "Test any degreaser on a small, inconspicuous area of the concrete first to check for discoloration."
  - "Check whether the concrete is sealed or unsealed, since sealed concrete resists staining better and cleans up more easily."
  - "Confirm nearby plants or grass won't be affected by degreaser runoff before rinsing."
dontDo:
  - "Don't use a pressure washer alone expecting it to remove set-in oil — it can spread the stain into a wider area without a degreaser first."
  - "Don't pour used degreaser or oil-soaked absorbent material down a storm drain — dispose of it properly per local regulations."
  - "Don't use straight bleach on oil stains — it doesn't break down oil and can discolor the concrete."
whenToCallPro:
  - "The stain has been present for years and DIY degreasing isn't making visible progress."
  - "The oil source is an active leak from a vehicle — get that repaired first, or any cleaning will be undone quickly."
  - "You're dealing with a large-scale spill that may need professional environmental cleanup, especially near storm drains."
faqs:
  - q: "Does WD-40 remove oil stains from concrete?"
    a: "Some people use it to help loosen very old, dried stains before degreasing, but it isn't a primary stain remover on its own and adds its own oily residue that still needs cleaning up."
  - q: "Will a pressure washer alone remove an oil stain?"
    a: "Not reliably for anything beyond a very fresh, light stain. Oil needs to be broken down chemically with a degreaser before pressure washing rinses it away."
  - q: "How do I stop new oil stains from forming?"
    a: "A concrete sealer applied to a clean, stain-free surface makes future spills much easier to wipe up before they soak in, and a drip pan under a leaking vehicle prevents new stains entirely."
