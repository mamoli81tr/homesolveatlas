quickAnswer: "Running out of hot water faster than you used to usually means either sediment buildup has reduced the tank's effective capacity, one of two heating elements has failed (electric tanks), or household hot-water demand has simply grown beyond what the tank was ever sized for."
symptoms:
  - "Hot water runs out noticeably faster than it used to"
  - "Water is lukewarm rather than fully cold when it 'runs out'"
  - "Recovery time between uses (waiting for hot water to return) has gotten longer"
  - "The problem is worse when multiple people shower or run appliances back to back"
causes:
  - title: "Sediment buildup reducing capacity"
    description: "Mineral sediment settling at the bottom of the tank takes up space that used to hold hot water, and also insulates the lower heating element from the water above it, slowing recovery."
  - title: "Failed lower heating element (electric tanks)"
    description: "Electric water heaters typically have two elements — upper and lower. If the lower one fails, you may still get some hot water (from the upper element) but capacity drops significantly, since only the top portion of the tank heats."
  - title: "Thermostat set too low"
    description: "A thermostat set conservatively low reduces the usable temperature swing before water feels lukewarm, effectively shrinking how much 'hot enough' water is available."
  - title: "Demand has outgrown tank size"
    description: "A tank that was adequate for a smaller household can become insufficient as more people, longer showers, or added appliances increase simultaneous hot-water demand."
safeChecks:
  - "Note whether water goes lukewarm gradually (suggests reduced capacity) or stays cold immediately (suggests a different issue, like a cross-connected pipe)."
  - "Check your water heater's rated tank size (usually on a label) against your household's size and typical usage."
  - "Check the thermostat setting — most manufacturers recommend 120°F (49°C) as a safe, adequate default."
  - "Note how many years it's been since the tank was last flushed."
steps:
  - title: "Flush the tank"
    description: "Follow a full drain-and-flush procedure (see our water heater popping noise guide for the step-by-step) to clear sediment that's reducing effective capacity."
  - title: "Check the thermostat setting"
    description: "Confirm it's set to a reasonable temperature (commonly 120°F/49°C) — too low reduces usable hot water, while too high raises scalding risk."
  - title: "Test both heating elements (electric tanks)"
    description: "A technician can test whether both the upper and lower elements are functioning — a failed lower element specifically causes a sharp, sudden reduction in hot water capacity."
  - title: "Reassess tank size against household needs"
    description: "If flushing and checking the thermostat don't resolve it, and the tank has always felt marginal, a larger tank or a tankless system may better match your household's actual demand."
dontDo:
  - "Don't set the thermostat above 120°F (49°C) without a specific reason — higher settings increase scalding risk and energy use."
  - "Don't ignore a sudden, sharp drop in capacity — that pattern (rather than a gradual decline) points to a failed heating element, not just gradual sediment buildup."
  - "Don't attempt electrical heating-element testing yourself unless you're confident safely de-energizing the circuit first."
whenToCallPro:
  - "You suspect a failed heating element (electric) or burner issue (gas) rather than simple sediment buildup."
  - "The tank is old (10+ years) and struggling — a technician can advise whether repair or replacement makes more sense."
  - "You're considering upsizing to a larger tank or switching to a tankless system and want a professional demand assessment."
faqs:
  - q: "How do I know if it's sediment or a failed element?"
    a: "A gradual decline over months usually points to sediment buildup. A sudden, sharp drop in hot water capacity is more characteristic of a failed heating element, especially on electric tanks with two elements."
  - q: "Would a tankless water heater solve this?"
    a: "Tankless systems provide continuous hot water rather than a fixed tank capacity, which can solve a 'runs out too fast' problem, but they have their own sizing requirements based on simultaneous demand (how many fixtures run at once) rather than total tank volume."
  - q: "Does flushing really make a noticeable difference?"
    a: "Yes, particularly if the tank hasn't been flushed in a year or more — sediment can meaningfully reduce both usable capacity and heating efficiency."
