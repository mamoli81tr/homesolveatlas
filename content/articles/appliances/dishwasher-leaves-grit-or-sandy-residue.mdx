quickAnswer: "A fine, sandy, or gritty texture — different from visible food chunks or a cloudy mineral film — most often means the filter has a tear or gap letting fine debris pass through and recirculate instead of being caught. Hard water combined with too little rinse aid is the other common contributor, especially if the grit feels slightly more like a chalky film than true sediment."
symptoms:
  - "Dishes feel gritty or sandy to the touch even though they look mostly clean"
  - "The feeling is more textural than a visible film or spots"
  - "Grit may be more noticeable on flatware and glass than on plates"
  - "The dishwasher's filter looks intact at a glance but may have a less obvious tear"
causes:
  - title: "Damaged or torn filter"
    description: "A crack, tear, or gap in the filter assembly — even a small one — lets fine food debris and sediment pass through and recirculate onto dishes instead of being caught and flushed away."
  - title: "Hard water combined with insufficient rinse aid"
    description: "In hard water areas, mineral content can leave a gritty or slightly rough feel distinct from the cloudier film covered in our white residue guide — rinse aid is specifically designed to counter this."
  - title: "Sediment in the household water supply"
    description: "Less common, but some homes (especially on well water) can have fine sediment entering with the water supply itself, independent of anything inside the dishwasher."
  - title: "Filter not seated correctly after cleaning"
    description: "If the filter was removed for cleaning and not fully reseated or locked back into place, it may not catch debris as effectively even without being damaged."
safeChecks:
  - "Remove the filter and inspect it closely for cracks, tears, or gaps, not just visible dirt."
  - "Confirm the filter is fully seated and locked in its normal position, not sitting loosely."
  - "Check your rinse aid dispenser and refill it if low, especially if you're in a known hard-water area."
  - "Compare the texture to a description of mineral film (smoother, more of a cloudy coating) versus true grit (finer, sand-like) to help narrow the cause."
steps:
  - title: "Inspect the filter closely for damage"
    description: "Look beyond just cleaning it — check the mesh and housing for any crack or tear, holding it up to light if helpful."
  - title: "Reseat the filter fully"
    description: "Remove and reinstall it according to your manual's instructions, confirming it locks or twists fully into place rather than sitting loose."
  - title: "Top up rinse aid"
    description: "Fill the rinse aid dispenser fully and run a normal cycle to see if the texture improves, particularly if you're in a hard-water area."
  - title: "Replace a damaged filter"
    description: "If you find a genuine tear or crack, a filter is a defined, generally accessible replacement part rather than something to repair."
dontDo:
  - "Don't confuse this with a cloudy mineral film — check our white residue guide if the issue looks more like haze than actual grit, since the fixes differ."
  - "Don't ignore a cracked filter thinking it will still catch debris adequately — even a small gap is enough to let fine particles through consistently."
  - "Don't overfill or add extra rinse aid beyond the dispenser's capacity trying to fix this faster."

faqs:
  - q: "How is this different from the white film on my glasses?"
    a: "White residue is typically a cloudy, smoother mineral coating from hard water, addressed with rinse aid and occasional descaling. Grit or sandy residue is a distinct, finer texture usually tied to a filter that's letting debris through rather than a coating issue — see our dedicated white residue guide if that description fits better."
  - q: "Can a small crack in the filter really cause this?"
    a: "Yes — even a small tear is enough to let fine debris bypass the filtering system consistently, since it doesn't need to be a large gap to let sediment-sized particles through."
  - q: "Does rinse aid help with actual grit, not just film?"
    a: "It primarily helps with hard-water mineral deposits rather than true food-debris grit, which is why checking the filter for damage is the more important step if rinse aid alone doesn't help."

The texture itself is the best clue here — a smoother, cloudy film points toward hard water and belongs with our white residue guide, while a genuinely gritty or sandy feel points more specifically at the filter.

| What you notice | Most likely cause |
|---|---|
| Fine, sand-like grit, especially on flatware | Torn or damaged filter |
| More of a cloudy film than true grit | See our white residue guide instead |
| You're in a known hard-water area | Insufficient rinse aid |
| Filter looks fine but was recently removed for cleaning | Not fully reseated |
