quickAnswer: "A weak or slow spin that leaves clothes noticeably wetter than usual is a different fault from a complete non-spin — the motor and drum are moving, just not reaching full speed. A slipping drive belt and worn motor carbon brushes are the two most common causes."
symptoms:
  - "The drum visibly spins but at a noticeably lower speed than normal"
  - "Clothes come out heavier and wetter than a typical finished cycle"
  - "The spin may start strong and then bog down partway through"
  - "A burning-rubber smell can accompany a slipping belt"
causes:
  - title: "Slipping drive belt"
    description: "A belt that's stretched, glazed (shiny and slick), or partially worn can grip enough to turn the drum slowly without reaching the speed needed for proper water extraction."
  - title: "Worn motor carbon brushes"
    description: "On motors that use carbon brushes, these wear down over years of use, gradually reducing the motor's power and top speed rather than causing a sudden complete failure."
  - title: "Persistent unbalanced load triggering a reduced-speed safety mode"
    description: "Some machines automatically cap spin speed when they detect an imbalance they can't fully correct, rather than refusing to spin outright."
  - title: "Overloading the machine"
    description: "A load packed beyond the machine's rated capacity doesn't have room to distribute and spin efficiently, resulting in a weak, ineffective spin regardless of mechanical condition."
safeChecks:
  - "Try a smaller, well-balanced load and see if spin speed and water extraction improve — this rules out overloading and load-balance issues quickly."
  - "Listen for a burning-rubber smell during spin, which points toward a slipping belt."
  - "Check the load size against your machine's rated capacity in the manual."
  - "Unplug the machine before doing any closer inspection of the belt."
steps:
  - title: "Test with a smaller, balanced load"
    description: "Run a small, evenly distributed load through a spin cycle to see if the problem is load-related rather than mechanical."
  - title: "Inspect the drive belt"
    description: "With power off, access the belt (location varies by model) and check for glazing, cracking, or looseness — a belt that looks shiny and slick rather than matte and textured is a strong sign of slipping."
  - title: "Consider motor brush wear if the belt looks fine"
    description: "If the belt is in good condition and load size isn't the issue, gradually reduced spin speed over months points toward worn carbon brushes, which is a job best handled by a technician."
  - title: "Replace the belt if worn"
    description: "A slipping belt is a defined, relatively inexpensive part to replace, though access varies by model — some are straightforward, others need more disassembly."
dontDo:

  - "Don't assume a weak spin means the whole machine needs replacing — belts and brushes are both routine wear items with defined fixes."

faqs:
  - q: "Is a slow spin the same problem as no spin at all?"
    a: "No — they usually point to different causes. A complete non-spin often means a safety switch, a fully broken belt, or a disconnected motor coupling. A weak, slow spin usually means something is still working but not at full strength, like a slipping (not broken) belt or worn motor brushes."
  - q: "Can overloading really cause this on its own?"
    a: "Yes — a load packed beyond capacity doesn't have room to distribute evenly, and many machines will deliberately limit spin speed rather than risk excessive vibration with a load that heavy."
  - q: "How long do drive belts typically last?"
    a: "It varies significantly with usage and machine model, and manufacturers don't publish a fixed replacement interval — treat visible glazing, cracking, or a burning smell during spin as the signal to check it, rather than a calendar date."
