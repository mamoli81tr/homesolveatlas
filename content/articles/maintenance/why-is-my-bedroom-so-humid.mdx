quickAnswer: "Bedrooms usually get humid from a combination of poor airflow, moisture from breathing and body heat overnight, and nearby sources like an en-suite bathroom or drying laundry — a hygrometer will confirm the actual humidity level in minutes."
symptoms:
  - "Windows fog up overnight or in the morning"
  - "A musty smell, especially in closets or corners"
  - "Bedding or curtains feel slightly damp to the touch"
  - "Visible condensation on windows, mirrors, or cold surfaces"
causes:
  - title: "Limited airflow and closed doors overnight"
    description: "A closed bedroom door with the HVAC vent also closed or restricted traps the moisture two adults produce simply by breathing and sweating through the night."
  - title: "An attached or nearby bathroom"
    description: "Steam from showers travels through doorways and shared walls more than people expect, especially without an extractor fan running long enough."
  - title: "Drying laundry indoors"
    description: "Air-drying clothes in or near the bedroom releases a significant amount of water vapor directly into that space."
  - title: "General home humidity levels"
    description: "In humid climates or during certain seasons, whole-home humidity can run high enough that any room without dedicated ventilation feels noticeably damp."
safeChecks:
  - "Use an inexpensive hygrometer to measure the room's actual relative humidity — above 50–55% is generally considered too high for comfort and mold prevention."
  - "Check that the HVAC vent in the room is open and not blocked by furniture."
  - "Check whether an en-suite bathroom's extractor fan runs long enough after showers (aim for 20–30 minutes)."
  - "Look at closet corners and behind furniture against exterior walls for early mold or musty smells."
steps:
  - title: "Measure current humidity"
    description: "Place a hygrometer in the room for a day to get a baseline reading, ideally checking both daytime and overnight levels."
  - title: "Improve airflow"
    description: "Open the bedroom door partway overnight if practical, and make sure furniture isn't blocking the HVAC vent or any window trickle vents."
  - title: "Address nearby moisture sources"
    description: "Run the bathroom extractor fan longer after showers, and move laundry drying to a well-ventilated space like a laundry room or outdoors."
  - title: "Use a dehumidifier if levels stay high"
    description: "A small portable dehumidifier sized for the room can bring persistent humidity down to a comfortable, mold-resistant range."
  - title: "Recheck after a week"
    description: "Measure again after making changes to confirm humidity has dropped into the 40–50% comfortable range."
dontDo:
  - "Don't ignore condensation on windows for weeks — it eventually leads to mold growth on frames and nearby walls."
  - "Don't block HVAC vents with furniture, even temporarily — it disrupts airflow throughout the connected duct system."
  - "Don't rely solely on scented sprays to mask a musty smell — address the moisture source instead."
whenToCallPro:
  - "Humidity stays above 60% consistently even after improving ventilation and using a dehumidifier."
  - "You find visible mold growth on walls, ceiling, or window frames."
  - "The room has an unexplained water stain or damp patch that could indicate a hidden leak rather than just air humidity."
faqs:
  - q: "What humidity level is considered too high for a bedroom?"

  - q: "Will a houseplant help reduce bedroom humidity?"
    a: "Some plants add moisture to a room rather than removing it, so they're not an effective fix for a humidity problem — a dehumidifier is far more reliable."
  - q: "Can heating the room reduce humidity?"
    a: "Warmer air can hold more moisture without condensing, which can help reduce visible condensation, but it doesn't reduce the actual moisture in the air the way ventilation or a dehumidifier does."
