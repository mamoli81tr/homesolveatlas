quickAnswer: "Condensation on the inside of window glass forms when warm, humid indoor air touches the cold glass surface, especially in winter. Occasional light condensation is normal; heavy or constant condensation signals excess indoor humidity worth addressing."
symptoms:
  - "Visible water droplets or fog on the inside of window glass, usually worse in the morning"
  - "Water pooling on the windowsill"
  - "Musty smell or early mold spots on the frame or nearby wall"
  - "Condensation that's worse on some windows (typically single-pane or older) than others"
causes:
  - title: "High indoor relative humidity"
    description: "Cooking, showering, drying laundry, and even breathing add moisture to indoor air, which condenses on the coldest surfaces in the home — usually window glass."
  - title: "Single-pane or older windows"
    description: "Windows with less insulating value have colder interior glass surfaces, so they show condensation at humidity levels that wouldn't affect a modern double-pane window."
  - title: "Poor air circulation near windows"
    description: "Heavy curtains or furniture blocking airflow around a window keep that immediate area colder and more humid than the rest of the room."
  - title: "Cold outdoor temperatures"
    description: "The bigger the temperature difference between inside and outside, the more likely condensation is to form, which is why this is most common in winter."
safeChecks:
  - "Wipe down affected windowsills regularly to prevent standing water from damaging wood frames or promoting mold."
  - "Measure indoor humidity with a hygrometer — above 50% in winter often leads to window condensation."
  - "Check whether condensation appears between two panes of a double-glazed window (rather than on the inside surface), which points to a seal failure, not a humidity issue."
steps:
  - title: "Reduce moisture at the source"
    description: "Use extractor fans while cooking and showering, cover pots while boiling, and vent your dryer outdoors rather than indoors."
  - title: "Improve air circulation near windows"
    description: "Pull curtains back slightly during the day and avoid pressing furniture directly against exterior walls or windows."
  - title: "Ventilate briefly, even in winter"
    description: "Opening a window for 5–10 minutes a day, even in cold weather, exchanges humid indoor air for drier outdoor air and resets condensation buildup."
  - title: "Consider a dehumidifier for persistent cases"
    description: "If condensation remains heavy despite ventilation changes, a dehumidifier sized for the affected room's square footage can bring humidity down reliably."
dontDo:
  - "Don't ignore standing water on a windowsill for long periods — it can warp wood frames and promote mold growth."
  - "Don't seal a home too tightly without adequate ventilation — reducing all airflow can trap humidity rather than reduce it."
  - "Don't assume all condensation is a humidity issue — condensation between glass panes is a window seal problem, not something ventilation will fix."
whenToCallPro:
  - "Condensation appears between two panes of a double-glazed window — this means the seal has failed and typically requires window repair or replacement."
  - "You see mold developing on the frame, sill, or nearby wall despite addressing humidity."
  - "Condensation is severe enough that water is running down walls or damaging flooring."
faqs:
  - q: "Is some window condensation normal in winter?"
    a: "Yes, light condensation on colder mornings is common, especially with older windows. It becomes a concern when it's heavy, constant, or leading to standing water and mold."
  - q: "Will a dehumidifier fix condensation in every room?"
    a: "A dehumidifier addresses whole-room or whole-home humidity, but a room with poor insulation or very cold glass may still show some condensation even at moderate humidity levels."
  - q: "Does condensation mean my windows need replacing?"
    a: "Not necessarily — condensation on the inside surface is usually a humidity and ventilation issue. Condensation trapped between panes, however, does indicate a seal failure worth addressing."
